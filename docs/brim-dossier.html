0Method and labels

Three labels run through this document, and they mean different things:

Fact Verified against a cited source, dated. Inference My reasoning from those facts, not itself sourced. Recommendation A judgement call I am making for Brim.

Where the research contradicts the brief you supplied, I say so and give the reason rather than quietly reconciling it. Three parts of the brief do get overturned below: the energy forecasting framing, the breadth of the privacy-cleanup and system-maintenance surface, and the implied role of the intelligence layer.

One structural note on the sequencing. The brief lists deep uninstall first and the safety model fifth. I have swapped them. Everything Brim does that is consequential — uninstalling, reclaiming, resetting, disabling background items — is an instance of the same underlying problem: proving that a file belongs to a thing before acting on it. If that model is right, the features are straightforward. If it is wrong, no amount of feature work saves the product. So it comes second, immediately after the market.

The 2025–26 shakeout, which changes the answer

Three events in the last twelve months matter more than any feature comparison.

Fact Pearcleaner is on hold indefinitely. The strongest open-source competitor — 14.6k GitHub stars, 374 forks, version 5.4.3, covering uninstall, orphan search, a development-environment manager, Homebrew, package receipts, services and an app updater — carries a maintainer notice that development stopped at the end of 2025. The reason is mundane and instructive: a job change, other priorities, and loss of access to a Mac to build on. Issues, pull requests and releases are paused.1 Its licence, Apache 2.0 with the Commons Clause, prohibits monetisation, which foreclosed the obvious route to sustaining it.1

Fact MacUpdater is dead. CoreCode discontinued it on 1 January 2026 after eight years. It supported real-time updates for about 6,700 apps and tracked version information for over 100,000 more. The stated reason was not technical: they could not build a sustainable non-subscription model, did no marketing, failed to enter the enterprise market, and declined on principle to sell subscription software.67 A free offline build remains, without database updates.6

Fact MacPaw is contracting at the edges. Setapp Mobile closed on 16 February 2026, and CleanMyMac Business — the Jamf-distributed fleet product launched in January 2025 — closes on 14 July 2026.98 For scale: MacPaw's reported 2024 revenue was about $90M, roughly 80% of it from CleanMyMac.10

Inference The category leader is a ~$70M/year consumer product retreating from anything that requires deep per-customer engineering. The best free alternative is frozen. The one tool that solved update management is gone and its technology is for sale. That is an unusual configuration: high demand, a weakened incumbent, and a vacated adjacent category — all at once.

The complaint underneath all the others

The most useful single piece of evidence I found is not a feature comparison. In the comment thread under Howard Oakley's January 2025 essay on macOS cleanup, a reader reports running Pearcleaner, finding roughly 3 GB of leftovers, deleting the ones that were obviously safe — and leaving the rest, because they could not tell whether they were false positives.2

Inference That is the market. Detection is solved well enough that a free tool finds gigabytes. Adjudication is not solved at all, so users abandon most of the result. Every tool competes on how much it finds; none competes on how much the user is willing to act on. The conversion rate from "found" to "removed with confidence" is the actual product metric, and nobody is optimising it.

Oakley's own position sharpens it. He argues the problem is properly the developer's to solve — an app should declare what it installs outside its bundle — and notes that name-based searching fails because many components deliberately do not carry the app's name.2 He also records the real harm, which is not wasted disk: leftover launch daemons and agents from incompletely removed software, security software especially, cause instability and occasionally kernel panics.2

Three positioning options

A. Better CleanMyMacB. Maintained PearcleanerC. The machine's application record
PromiseMore gigabytes, nicer UI, no subscription gamesSame jobs, actively developed, more polishKnow what every app installed, what it is doing, and what happens when it goes
Real usefulnessModerate — buyers already distrust the claimHigh for the existing audience; that audience is small and unwilling to payHigh if adjudication genuinely works; unproven
DifferentiationWeak. Competes on marketing spend against a $70M productWeak. Competes on stamina against freeStrong if executed; nobody currently sells confidence
FeasibilityHighHighMedium — depends on evidence tiers holding up (§2)
MaintenanceGrows with rules and heuristicsGrows with surface areaGrows with macOS's own interfaces, which change slowly

Challenge to C

"Confidence" is not a thing people search for. Nobody types Mac tool that explains its reasoning into a search bar; they type free up space on Mac. C risks being a beautifully argued product that never gets found, sold against a competitor whose messaging is one number and one button. There is also a harder problem: a user who cannot judge whether ~/Library/Application Support/Vendor is safe to delete probably cannot judge Brim's explanation of it either. Explanations may simply relocate the anxiety.

And the hidden assumption in C is that users want a record. Most users want a result. A record is what a technician wants.

Re-evaluation

The challenge kills C as marketing copy and leaves it standing as architecture. The fix is to separate the two. The record is how Brim works; what it sells is the outcome that record makes possible — removals that are complete, reversible, and verified afterwards; space that actually comes back; a laptop that stops losing an hour of battery to something the user never chose to run.

On the second objection: an explanation that requires judgement has failed. The evidence model has to produce a default the user can accept without reading, with the reasoning available underneath for the minority who look. Confidence tiers do this. The user's decision becomes "accept Brim's defaults" rather than "adjudicate 40 files".

B is not worthless either. With Pearcleaner frozen, there is a real, warm audience of tens of thousands who will try a credible successor on day one. That is distribution, not positioning.

Decision

Position Brim as C internally, and as outcomes externally: "Remove an app completely, and see proof that it's gone." Use B as the launch wedge — be visibly the maintained, native, no-subscription successor that the Pearcleaner audience is currently looking for — and let the record-keeping become visible as users go deeper.

Do not compete with CleanMyMac on gigabyte counts. That is the one fight where the incumbent's weakness — inflated, unverifiable numbers — becomes a strength, because Brim's honest number will always be smaller.

2Ownership, evidence and the safety model

The load-bearing section. Everything consequential in Brim reduces to a claim of the form "this file belongs to that app."

Why naïve matching fails, concretely

Fact In 2013 a MacPaw engineer posted publicly on Adobe's forums asking for help: uninstalling one Adobe product with CleanMyMac was corrupting others, apparently because the products shared resources.21 That is the canonical failure and it has never gone away — Adobe, Microsoft, Steam, Electron apps with shared framework caches, and anything using group containers all have files that more than one app depends on.

Fact Oakley's rebuttal to a reader's 40-line find script is that it would not even catch his own app's files, because components frequently do not use the app's name, for good reasons.2 Name matching has both false positives and false negatives, and they are not symmetric in cost.

Three evidence models

CoverageWide, shallowDeep for covered apps, zero otherwiseWide, with coverage stated honestly per item
SafetyPoor. This is the Adobe failure modeGood where curatedGood by construction — strength of proof is the selection criterion
MaintenanceLow, but every bug report adds an exceptionVery high — MacUpdater died of exactly this cost class6Low. The proof sources are OS interfaces, not per-app knowledge

The tiers

Recommendation Four tiers, assigned per candidate path, visible in the interface.

TierEvidenceDefault
A — DeclaredInstaller receipt bill of materials for .pkg-installed software (pkgutil, lsbom, /var/db/receipts); sandbox container at ~/Library/Containers/<bundle-id>; group containers named in the app's entitlements; SMAppService items declared inside the bundle; Background Task Management records associated to the bundle; Launch Services registrationSelected
B — AttributedPath component is an exact bundle-identifier match; property list or binary signed by the same Team ID; a launchd job whose program path resolves inside the bundle; HTTPStorages, Saved Application State, Application Scripts keyed by bundle IDSelected
C — CorrelatedVendor-name folder, product-name folder, filename similarity, timestamps clustering with install timeNot selected. Shown, individually selectable, never bulk-selected
S — SharedClaimed by two or more installed apps, referenced by a receipt belonging to another installed product, or inside a group container shared by a still-present appExcluded, with the other claimant named

Inference Tier S is the single most valuable line in this table and the cheapest to build. It is a set intersection over data Brim already has. It is also precisely what would have prevented the Adobe incident.

Reversibility and verification

Three layers, in order of cost:

  • Trash, never unlink. Everything removable goes through trashItem. Now table stakes1, but the root-owned items a privileged helper touches are the ones that need it most and are hardest to do.
  • A removal ledger. A durable local record per operation: every path, its size, its tier, the evidence string, the timestamp, the app it belonged to, and whether removal succeeded. This is the undo source, the audit trail, and — exported — the receipt the user can keep.
  • Verification. After the operation, re-scan and report what remains, and measure the filesystem's free space before and after with statfs, reporting the real delta. When the delta is zero because blocks are pinned by a snapshot, say so (§9).

Challenge to the tiered model

Four attacks, and the fourth is serious.

Coverage collapse. Tier A depends on installer receipts, but most Mac apps are drag-installed and have no receipt at all. Tier B depends on bundle identifiers appearing in paths, which is exactly the assumption Oakley says fails. Strip out Tier C and Brim may find dramatically less than AppCleaner, and users will read that as a worse product.

Tier S under-counts. Shared usage is often invisible on disk: two apps reading the same support folder leaves no artefact unless one of them declares it. Brim will mark things unshared that are shared.

Ledger integrity. An undo record that points at Trash items the user has since emptied is a promise Brim cannot keep.

Permissions make tiers partly unenforceable. TCC protects ~/Library/Containers and ~/Library/Group Containers, and root does not bypass it — sudo rm returns "Operation not permitted".17 Apple's own guidance distinguishes EPERM (a privacy denial) from EACCES (a POSIX one) for exactly this reason.16 Worse, macOS 27 extends container-style protection to a hand-picked set of non-sandboxed apps' ~/Library/Application Support/<name> folders, and has moved the user TCC database into a protected system location.18 Brim can have perfect evidence and still be unable to act.

Re-evaluation

The coverage objection is real but misdiagnosed. Brim does not have to find less — Tier C candidates are still found and shown, just not pre-selected. The headline number is "48 items, 1.9 GB", with "41 confirmed, 7 need your call". That is more information than AppCleaner gives, not less.

Tier S under-counting is a genuine limit and the honest response is asymmetry: Tier S is a one-way veto. It can only move items out of the default selection, never into it. Missing a shared file leaves it at Tier B or C, where the existing defaults already apply.

The ledger objection argues for recording the Trash destination path and verifying it still exists before offering undo, and for saying "no longer recoverable" rather than failing.

The permissions objection is the one that changes the design. It means Brim must treat capability as a first-class part of the evidence display: not just "this belongs to the app" but "and here is whether I can remove it, and what you must do if I can't". This is a better product than one that silently fails, and macOS 27's direction of travel means it will matter more every year, not less.

Decision

Build the tiered evidence graph. Ship Tier S as a hard veto from the first release. Store a removal ledger with evidence strings and verify free-space deltas after every operation.

Add one thing the challenge surfaced: a capability check per item, computed before the operation, not discovered during it. The interface distinguishes "will remove", "needs the helper", "needs Full Disk Access", and "macOS will not permit this — here is the vendor's uninstaller instead".

Reject the curated rules database outright. It is the cost structure that killed MacUpdater,6 and it cannot explain itself.

3Deep uninstall and application footprint

Three strategies

A. Scan at removal timeB. Observe installs continuouslyC. Declared manifests
MechanismEvidence graph over the filesystem when the user asksA background watcher records what appears after each install; Pearcleaner's Sentinel watches the Trash in ~2 MB of RAM1Apps ship a property list declaring what they install outside the bundle — Oakley's proposal2
Covers existing appsYesNo — only apps installed after BrimNo — only apps that adopt it
AccuracyTier-limitedVery high for what it seesPerfect where present
Cost to userNoneA resident process; readers openly distrust always-on watchers2None
Ecosystem dependencyNoneNoneTotal. No adoption today

Challenge to A, the obvious winner

A inherits every weakness of §2 and adds one. It is retrospective: at removal time the app's own installer log, download quarantine attributes and installation timestamp context are gone or stale. A is structurally incapable of finding a file that shares nothing with the app — no name, no bundle ID, no receipt, no Team ID — and such files demonstrably exist.

The temptation is then to add B and get the accuracy back. But B's honest description is "a daemon that logs what you install." In the same thread where Oakley proposes manifests, a reader explains why they leave Nektony's watcher off: they do not know what a permanently resident third-party process is doing, and Oakley calls that suspicion reasonable.2 For a product whose entire proposition is trustworthiness, shipping an always-on observer as a default is a contradiction.

Re-evaluation

B's problem is the daemon, not the idea. Most of B's value comes from a much weaker mechanism: record the state of the known install locations periodically and on app-launch, and diff. Brim is already enumerating those paths for its index (§14). If Brim takes a snapshot of the install surface on each run, a newly appeared app's footprint is derivable by subtraction without watching anything in real time. No daemon, no install-time hooking, no privacy story to defend.

C is not buildable by Brim alone, but it is publishable by Brim alone, and that is a different and much cheaper proposition. A small open specification — an uninstall-manifest.plist with mandatory and optional entries, exactly as Oakley sketched2 — plus Brim reading it when present and Brim's own bundle shipping one, costs a few days. If even a handful of indie developers adopt it, Brim becomes the reference implementation of the ecosystem's fix. If nobody adopts it, Brim loses nothing.

Decision

Ship A as the engine. Add differential footprint tracking — periodic snapshots of the install surface, diffed — as the accuracy layer, with no resident process. Publish the manifest specification, read it when present, and lead by example.

Explicitly reject the always-running install watcher for V1. It is the single feature most likely to undermine the trust the rest of the product is built on. Revisit only as an opt-in, clearly explained, with its exact behaviour documented.

What "footprint" should contain

The brief's list is right and worth making concrete. For each app: the bundle and its real on-disk size; containers and group containers; preferences including ByHost; application support; caches and HTTPStorages; saved application state; logs and crash reports; launch agents and daemons in all three domains; login and background items; privileged helper tools; system and app extensions; command-line tools in /usr/local/bin and /opt; installer receipts; quarantine and Gatekeeper status; Team ID and signing state; and the TCC permissions the app currently holds.

Recommendation Add one item nobody shows: the app's privacy grants. On removal, offer tccutil reset scoped to the bundle identifier. A removed app that still holds camera, microphone, accessibility or full-disk entries in the privacy database is a real residue with real consequences, and it is invisible in every competing tool.

4Leftover and orphan detection

The definition problem

"Orphan" sounds simple and is not. A file whose owning app is absent from /Applications may be: genuinely abandoned; belonging to an app in another user's account; belonging to an app on an external or network volume; belonging to a command-line tool with no bundle at all; or belonging to an app the user will reinstall next week and would like to keep settings for.

A. Present-tense matchingB. Historical ledgerC. Behavioural ageing
RuleResidue with a bundle ID not currently installedResidue whose owner Brim previously recorded as installed and has since seen removedRank by last-access time, size, and whether anything has opened the file
PrecisionLow — every false negative in app discovery becomes a false orphanHighMedium
Works on first runYesNoYes
CostFreeRequires the ledger to exist and persistCheap; getattrlistbulk returns access times in the same pass

Challenge to the historical ledger

B is correct and nearly useless at the moment of maximum value. A user installs Brim precisely because their machine is already cluttered — often a machine migrated across several Macs, which Oakley identifies as the worst case for inherited clutter.2 On day one, B has no history and answers nothing. It only becomes good after months, by which time the user has already judged the product.

There is also a quieter failure: last-access time is unreliable on macOS. Backup software, indexing, antivirus and Brim's own scanning can all touch files. Ranking by "not opened in two years" invites false confidence.

Re-evaluation

The day-one gap is real, which means A must carry the first run — but A's precision problem is fixable by widening the search for owners rather than by weakening the rule. Before declaring anything orphaned, check: all mounted volumes, other user accounts that are readable, Launch Services' registration database, and installer receipts (a receipt for an absent product is strong evidence of removal rather than of an undiscovered app).

Where ownership is genuinely unresolvable, the honest label is not "orphaned". It is unclaimed, and the difference should be visible. An item Brim watched an app leave behind is a different kind of fact from an item Brim simply cannot attribute.

On access times: use them to sort, never to justify. Size, and the presence of a confirmed removal event, carry the decision.

Decision

Two categories, never merged. Orphaned — the owner was recorded present and is now gone, or an installer receipt exists for an absent product. Safe to pre-select. Unclaimed — residue Brim cannot attribute to any installed app, after checking all volumes and accounts. Shown, sorted by size, never pre-selected.

Start the ledger on first launch even though it has no history yet, and say so in the empty state: the value compounds, and being explicit about that is more honest than a fabricated day-one result.

5Application lifecycle management

The brief's strongest original idea is treating an app as an entity with a life, not an item in a list. Three ways to express that:

A. Inventory with actionsB. Timeline per appC. State machine
ModelA list; each row offers uninstall, reset, update, revealEach app has a history: first seen, versions, what it registered, how it grewApps occupy explicit states: active, dormant, stale, orphaned, quarantined
User valueImmediate, familiarAnswers "when did this start?" and "what changed?"Drives good default triage
CostLowMedium — needs the ledger and snapshotsLow on top of B
RiskIndistinguishable from competitorsFeels like surveillance if framed badlyMislabelling an app "stale" is annoying and wrong

Challenge to the timeline

A per-app history is the thing that makes Brim structurally different from every competitor, and it is also the thing most likely to feel creepy. "Brim noticed you haven't opened this in 11 months" is one sentence away from "Brim is watching what you use." The information is stored locally and never leaves the machine, but users do not audit storage; they react to tone.

It also has a cold-start problem identical to §4 and a correctness problem: last-used date on macOS is soft, and a dormant-looking app may be a dependency of something the user very much needs — a driver panel, a licence manager, an audio plug-in host.

Re-evaluation

The framing fix is to make the timeline about the app, not about the user. "Installed 14 March. Registered a launch daemon on 2 April. Support data grew from 40 MB to 2.1 GB." — that is a record of software behaviour, which is exactly what Brim promises to surface, and it never says what the person did.

The dormancy risk argues for demoting "unused app" from a recommendation to a sort order, and for never pre-selecting an app for removal on dormancy alone. Growth, background activity and energy cost are better signals than last-used, because they describe what the app is doing now.

Decision

Build B, worded as a record of the software. Layer C on top but use it only for grouping and sorting. Never auto-select an app for removal.

Two lifecycle actions deserve first-class treatment beyond uninstall, because they are underserved everywhere:

Reset. Return an app to first-run state — remove preferences, caches, saved state and container data while keeping the bundle and, critically, keeping licence and keychain material where it can be identified. This is the fix for a large share of "app is broken" problems and today requires manual path surgery. It uses exactly the same evidence graph as uninstall with a different selection filter, so it is nearly free to build.

Archive. Export the bundle plus its data and a manifest before removal, so removal stops being a one-way door. Pearcleaner proved the demand with app-bundle export;1 pairing it with the removal ledger makes it a genuine restore path.

6Updates

The area where the research most clearly overturns the obvious plan.

Fact MacUpdater tracked over 100,000 apps with real-time support for about 6,700, required daily database maintenance, had tens of thousands of paying customers, and shut down because the model could not be sustained without a subscription its authors declined to sell.67 Fact MacUpdater itself used Homebrew Cask data to support automatic updates for thousands of apps that lack their own updater.24 Fact Sparkle's appcast is the de-facto standard for non-App-Store updates and is explicitly designed to be readable by third-party update trackers; Latest drives updates through Sparkle with its own user interface.2325

A. Own version databaseB. Read the sources already in the systemC. Detect and route only
CoverageHighest possibleSparkle feeds + Homebrew Cask + Mac App Store. Broad but not universalSame detection, no installation
Ongoing costDaily, forever, by a humanNear zero — the maintenance is someone else's, and already fundedNear zero
Failure modeBusiness death6Silent gaps where an app uses neither mechanismSame gaps, plus more user work
Risk of breaking appsMediumLow — each update is performed by the app's own mechanismNone

Challenge to B

B's coverage gap is not evenly distributed. The apps most likely to lack both a Sparkle feed and a Homebrew cask are exactly the awkward ones — enterprise agents, vendor-installed drivers, creative suites with their own updaters — which are also the ones most likely to be outdated and to matter for security. Brim will show a clean update list while the genuinely stale software sits outside it.

There is a second risk. Reading a Sparkle appcast means making a network request to a third-party URL taken from an app's property list. For a product whose privacy claim is "no network by default", that is a meaningful boundary crossing, and the appcast URL is attacker-influenced input if an app bundle has been tampered with.

And updating an app means replacing a bundle that may be signed, quarantined, running, or protected. Doing that badly is how a utility destroys someone's install.

Re-evaluation

The coverage gap argues for stating it rather than hiding it. Brim should show, per app, how it can check: "Sparkle feed", "Homebrew cask", "App Store", or "no automatic source — last checked never". An app with no source is itself a finding worth surfacing, and it is a finding no competitor reports.

The network objection is answered by consent and scoping, not avoidance: update checking is a user-initiated action, requests go only to URLs already present in installed bundles, and the feature can be turned off entirely. This is the one place Brim legitimately touches the network.

The installation risk argues for delegation: where Sparkle is present, drive Sparkle; where Homebrew manages the app, invoke brew; where it is App Store software, hand off to the App Store. Brim should never hand-roll a bundle replacement.

Decision

Build B, with C as the fallback for anything B cannot install. Never build A. State the source per app and treat "no update source" as a reportable condition.

Do not auto-install anything by default. The value here is knowing, not automating, and automatic replacement of signed bundles is the highest-blast-radius thing a utility can do.

Inference This is also a positioning gift. A credible update view that costs Brim nothing to maintain lands into a category that has been empty since 1 January 2026, and it can be advertised truthfully as "we don't run a database, so this can't go dark on you."

7Background and system item management

The most under-served problem on the platform, and the clearest 10× opportunity.

What the OS actually exposes

Fact macOS records login items, launch agents, launch daemons and service-management payloads in a Background Task Management database. Apple's deployment documentation names two commands for it: sfltool dumpbtm, which prints the full status of every item, and sfltool resetbtm, which resets the data.4 Both require root.5 Oakley documents both as undocumented options whose manual page has not caught up.3

Fact The System Settings panel is far less informative than the underlying data. One practitioner's machine showed 19 items in Settings and 40 in the dump — many of them entries from applications already uninstalled — and Settings shows no bundle identifier and no path.5 On Apple's own developer forums, an engineer diagnoses a case where uninstalling an app garbage-collected its agent but left its daemon registered.26

Fact The only remedy in circulation is the reset, which wipes every login and background item and requires a restart, after which apps may forget their settings.35

Inference This is an unusually clean gap. There is a real, widespread, user-visible problem — "Background Items Added" notifications for software that no longer exists — where the OS's own interface hides the diagnostic information, the fix is a blunt instrument, and no consumer tool does better. It is also directly continuous with Brim's uninstall story, because stale background items are precisely what incomplete uninstalls leave behind.

A. Parse the BTM dumpB. Supported APIs onlyC. On-disk job files only
Sourcesfltool dumpbtm via privileged helperSMAppService status, launchctl printLaunchAgents/LaunchDaemons in all domains
Sees stale entries for removed appsYes — the whole pointNoPartially
Stability across OS releasesLow — undocumented format3HighHigh
Can act on what it findsOnly via reset, or by removing the backing itemYes, for Brim's own itemsYes — unload and remove

Challenge to A

Building a headline feature on an undocumented text format printed by a tool whose manual page is out of date is a maintenance liability that recurs every September. Worse, parsing gives Brim visibility without agency: knowing that a phantom entry exists does not let Brim delete that specific record. The only supported lever remains the global reset, which is exactly the blunt instrument users are already unhappy with.

There is also a real hazard in offering the reset at all. It clears legitimate login items too, and apps may lose their "do not open at login" preference afterwards.5 A utility that recommends it casually will break working setups.

Re-evaluation

The agency objection is the important one, and it reframes the feature. Brim's contribution is not deleting BTM records; it is making the reset safe to use, which nobody has done. Concretely: dump and join the records to installed apps; show exactly which entries are phantoms and which are live; capture a complete restore list before the reset; perform the reset; then walk the user through re-enabling the items that should come back. The dangerous operation becomes a reviewed, reversible one.

Separately, for the common case where a phantom entry has a backing file still on disk, C acts directly: unload the job and remove the property list, which is supported, targeted and safe.

The fragility objection argues for layering. Parsing is an enrichment layer. If a future macOS changes the format, Brim degrades to B plus C — a worse feature, not a broken product. Nothing consequential should depend on the parse succeeding.

Decision

Build all three as layers: supported APIs for structure, on-disk job files for targeted action, BTM parsing for visibility. Feature them as one view answering "what runs on this Mac without being asked, and who put it there."

Ship the guided reset with a captured restore list. This is a small amount of code sitting on top of a problem the platform has left open for four years, and it is the kind of thing a reviewer writes about.

8Developer and system cleanup

Developer caches are the highest-yield, lowest-risk reclaimable data on a working Mac — Xcode derived data, archives, device support directories and simulator runtimes; npm, pnpm and Yarn stores; pip and uv caches; the Cargo registry and build directories; the Go module cache; Gradle and Maven; Homebrew's download cache; and container disk images.

Challenge to B

Delegation sounds unimpeachable but it is slow, it requires the tool to be installed and on a path Brim can find, and it hands control to a subprocess whose behaviour Brim does not define. brew cleanup, go clean -modcache and a container prune each do somewhat more than "free space", and a user who clicked a button in a cleanup app has not consented to a container prune's semantics. Delegation can be less predictable than deletion, not more.

And B cannot cover the biggest single item on many developer machines: a container runtime's virtual disk file, which is sparse, does not shrink when images are deleted, and cannot be safely truncated by an outside process.

Re-evaluation

The objection is about consent and predictability rather than about the mechanism. The resolution is to classify by what the reclamation actually costs the user, and to let the class determine the treatment:

  • Pure regenerable caches — derived data, download caches, package stores. Delete directly. Cost of being wrong is a slower next build.
  • Tool-managed stores with internal state — module caches with read-only permissions, Homebrew, package managers with lockfile-linked layouts. Delegate, and show the exact command before running it.
  • Stateful artefacts — container disk images, simulator devices holding app data, archives containing shipped builds' debug symbols. Report and route. Never act. Open the tool that owns them.

Decision

Three-class model. Show reclaimable size per class and the regeneration cost in plain words — "next build will be slower" versus "these are your only copies of the debug symbols for shipped builds".

Recommendation Keep this feature but do not let it expand into general system maintenance. Cache clearing, log rotation, maintenance-script running and permission repair are either handled by macOS already or are folklore. Oakley's position on the folder macOS controls is direct: caches and temporary directories are the system's job, and files land in places macOS empties itself.2 Brim including a "run maintenance scripts" button would import the exact credibility problem it is differentiating against.

9Storage analysis

Where the incumbents are most vulnerable, because their headline number is frequently not true.

The accounting problem

Fact On APFS, free space is not "total minus the sum of visible files". Copy-on-write means deleted files' blocks stay allocated while any snapshot still references them, so emptying the Trash can free nothing at all.22 Local Time Machine snapshots count as purgeable space, and macOS purges them itself when space is needed.20 Fact Purgeable accounting has been inconsistent and misleading since APFS shipped — Oakley has documented the same class of discrepancy repeatedly from 2017 through Tahoe 26.6, including cases where the system failed to recognise purgeable space that was available.20

Inference This is why "System Data" is the most-complained-about number on macOS and why cleaners are so often accused of not working. A tool quotes a recoverable figure, the user runs it, and the bar does not move — because the space was pinned, or was already purgeable and would have been reclaimed automatically anyway.

A. Treemap visualiserB. Junk-category cleanerC. Reconciled storage account
Question answered"What is biggest?""What can I delete?""Where did my space go, and what will actually come back?"
HonestyHigh but incomplete — treemaps rarely show snapshot-pinned or purgeable spaceLow — the headline figure is usually not deliverableHigh by construction
DifferentiationNone; DaisyDisk and GrandPerspective own thisNone; everyone ships itNobody does this

Challenge to C

C is intellectually right and commercially awkward. Its output is frequently "you cannot reclaim anything useful here, and the 200 GB you are worried about is snapshots that macOS will handle." That is the correct answer and a terrible sales pitch against a competitor promising 47 GB.

It also cannot deliver its best trick without cooperation from a system Brim does not control: snapshot purging is macOS's decision, and Oakley shows the accounting itself is sometimes wrong on Apple's side.20 Brim can explain the situation but cannot guarantee an outcome.

And there is a coverage trap. Large-file and duplicate finding is what users actually ask for, and C by itself does not provide it.

Re-evaluation

"You do not have a problem" is only a bad pitch if the product is sold on gigabytes. If it is sold on understanding, it is the payoff. The framing that survives is diagnostic: why doesn't deleting things free space? — a question with hundreds of thousands of search results and no good software answer.

C does not have to be alone. Treemap enumeration and duplicate detection are cheap once Brim has a fast enumerator for its index. The improvement Brim can make over the commodity versions is again accuracy: a duplicate finder that reports clone-linked files as recoverable space is lying, because APFS clones already share their blocks. Detecting shared extents and reporting real savings would put Brim ahead of most duplicate finders on the only axis that matters.

Decision

Build C as the storage feature, with a treemap and duplicate detection as supporting views rather than headlines. Three numbers, always shown together and never collapsed into one: logical size of what you see, reclaimable now, and held by snapshots or purgeable. Verify every reclamation with a measured free-space delta.

Duplicate detection: size filter, then head and tail hashes, then full content hash only on survivors — and exclude clone-linked pairs from the savings total, labelling them as already sharing storage.

10Energy and battery intelligence

The brief's most ambitious claim, and the one where the research most changes what is buildable.

What is actually measurable

Fact Activity Monitor's "Energy Impact" is explicitly a relative measure, not a physical quantity; laptops additionally show a 12-hour average.11 Practitioner analysis suggests it blends CPU usage and power draw, GPU use, power assertions, memory and network activity.27 Its underlying wakeup counters come from task_power_info via task_info.28

Fact powermetrics can print per-process energy impact with --show-process-energy, and can group processes into coalitions so that time from exited children is still attributed correctly.12 It requires root.

Fact The most useful finding: proc_pid_rusage with RUSAGE_INFO_V6 returns per-process cumulative counters including ri_energy_nj and ri_penergy_nj in nanojoules, alongside instructions, cycles and billed energy.13b Tools that read it report per-process energy without requiring root.14

UnitRelative impact scoreReal joules, per hardware blockReal joules (kernel's accounting), per process
Per-app attributionYes, with coalitionsNo — it is per component, not per processYes
PrivilegeRoot; a helper must run it continuouslyNone reported14None, for the user's own processes
StabilityCommand-line output parsingUndocumented private interfacePublic SDK header; semantics undocumented
Cost to runA resident root subprocessLowVery low — one syscall per process per sample

Challenge to C

Five problems, and they are not small.

Counters are cumulative from process start. Every quit and relaunch resets to zero. A week of history requires Brim to sample regularly and persist deltas — which means Brim needs to be running, which reintroduces the resident-process question from §3.

Process is not app. Modern apps are many processes — helpers, XPC services, web content renderers. Attribution requires coalition or bundle-path grouping, and getting it wrong makes a browser look free and its renderers look like the problem.

Root-owned processes are invisible without elevation, and daemons are exactly the background consumers the feature promises to expose.

It measures compute, not consequence. An app that keeps the display awake, holds a power assertion, pins the radio, or triggers the discrete GPU may cost far more battery than its own energy counter shows.

Forecasting is where this becomes indefensible. The brief asks for "projected future battery cost for continuously running applications". Projection requires stable behaviour, constant conditions and a known baseline. None hold: workloads vary, the OS reschedules discretionary work, thermal state changes, and battery capacity itself degrades. A forecast in minutes of battery life would be wrong often enough to destroy the credibility the rest of the product is built on.

Re-evaluation

Four of the five problems are answerable and one changes the feature.

Sampling cadence: energy counters are cumulative, so sampling can be slow — every few minutes is ample — and the sampler is tiny. That is a very different proposition from a filesystem watcher: it reads counters about processes, stores numbers locally, and has no access to content. It can be an opt-in that starts when the user first opens the energy view. Gaps while Brim is closed are acceptable if reported as coverage rather than hidden.

Attribution: group by coalition where possible and by bundle path otherwise, and show the breakdown. "Browser: 41%, of which renderers 33%" is more useful than a single figure anyway.

Root processes: read what is available without elevation, mark the rest as requiring the helper, and let the user decide.

Compute-versus-consequence: this is fixable by widening the display rather than the model. Power assertions are readable, and App Nap status and high-performance GPU use are already surfaced by the OS.11 Showing "holds a display-sleep assertion" next to an energy figure is more actionable than any refinement of the figure itself.

Forecasting does not survive as prediction. It survives as arithmetic the user can check: this app averaged X mWh per day over the last 14 days; your battery's design capacity is Y mWh; that is Z% of a full charge per day. Every term is measured, the operation is a division, and the user can audit it. That is a defensible version of the brief's intent.

Decision

Build on proc_pid_rusage. Report energy in real units and as a share of the battery's design capacity. Persist deltas locally with a small, opt-in sampler that the user starts by opening the feature, and show coverage honestly.

Overturn the brief on forecasting: ship observed cost expressed as a rate, with the arithmetic visible, and reject predicted battery life in minutes, hours or any modelled future. No trend extrapolation, no machine learning.

Add the assertion and GPU context alongside — it is cheap and it is what makes the number actionable.

Inference Done this way, this is the most defensible differentiator in the whole product. Activity Monitor has no memory, every competitor reports instantaneous impact, and "this app you never open cost you 11% of a charge this week" is a sentence nobody else can currently produce.

Challenge to C

C is obviously safest and obviously less marketable. Every competitor will ship an assistant; MacPaw is already testing one at scale.10 A product with no AI in 2026 looks like a product that could not build one, and there are real jobs a model does better than templates: summarising an unfamiliar vendor's footprint, answering "why is this running", and turning a question into a query over the local index.

The deeper objection is that C's premise — "the evidence graph already contains the explanation" — is only true if the explanations are genuinely readable. Rendering an evidence tier as a sentence is easy; making forty of them feel like an answer rather than a log is not.

Re-evaluation

The challenge does not dislodge C as the engine; it argues for a model as a presentation layer over facts Brim already has, which is the one use where hallucination risk is bounded. The distinction that matters is authority: a model may phrase, summarise and search. It may never decide, select, or authorise a deletion. If the model is removed, the product loses polish and loses nothing else.

The conditional availability of the on-device model settles the architecture on its own. A feature that is unavailable on ineligible hardware, in unsupported regions, or when the user has not enabled Apple Intelligence cannot be load-bearing. And WWDC 2026's provider protocol means a bring-your-own-key path later costs almost nothing, through the same native API.15

Decision

Deterministic explanations are the product. The model is optional, local-first, off by default, and confined to three jobs: plain-language summaries of a scan result, answering "what is this?" about a path or process from data already gathered, and natural-language search over the local index.

A hard constraint, stated in the interface: nothing the model produces can select a file for deletion or confirm a consequential action. Selection comes from the evidence tiers; confirmation comes from the person.

Overturn the brief's framing slightly. It describes the intelligence layer as explaining unusual resource usage and summarising cleanup opportunities "without replacing user control". That is the right instinct but the wrong boundary — the boundary should be that the layer never touches the decision path at all, not that a human approves its conclusions.

12Open source versus premium positioning

Three data points bound this decision. Pearcleaner is Apache 2.0 with the Commons Clause, which explicitly forbids monetisation of the project or derivatives — and it stalled when its maintainer's circumstances changed, with no mechanism to fund continuation.1 MacUpdater had tens of thousands of paying customers and died refusing a subscription.67 CleanMyMac is a closed, subscription-priced product generating roughly $70M a year.10

Challenge to C

If the engine is public, someone builds and distributes free binaries, and Brim competes with its own source. Worse, in this category that is not merely lost revenue — it is a safety problem, because a repackaged unsigned build of a tool that deletes system files is an ideal malware vector. This is not hypothetical: Pearcleaner's README warns that a domain impersonating it was distributing malware, with only one legitimate site.1

The second objection is that openness may buy nothing commercially. Almost no buyer reads source. They read reviews.

Re-evaluation

The impersonation risk argues for the model rather than against it, but changes what is emphasised. What buyers pay for is not bytes; it is a notarised, signed, single-origin build with a known publisher — which is precisely what the malware incident shows is scarce and valuable. Openness and paid distribution reinforce each other here: the source is the safety argument, the signature is the product.

The "nobody reads source" objection is true of individuals and false of the people who create demand for this category — the technically literate users who answer "what should I use instead of CleanMyMac?" in forums. That audience is the distribution channel, and auditability is the argument that wins them.

On licensing: the Commons Clause is a cautionary example, not a model. It blocked the maintainer's own path to sustainability while doing little to prevent repackaging.

Decision

Open the parts that constitute the safety claim — the evidence-tier rules, the path model, the ledger format, the manifest specification — under a permissive licence, so they can be audited, cited and adopted. Sell the assembled, signed, notarised application with support and updates.

Price it as a one-time purchase with paid major upgrades, not a subscription, unless a recurring cost emerges that genuinely scales with time. Following §6's architecture there is none, which is a structural advantage worth stating publicly: Brim runs no database, so it has no reason to need a subscription, and no way to go dark the way MacUpdater did.

Recommendation Register the obvious lookalike domains at launch and publish the signing identity prominently. Given what happened to Pearcleaner, this is a launch-day task, not a later one.

Decision

Two rules for the detail pane. Every removable item shows its evidence and its tier without a click. Every consequential action shows what will happen, what it costs to be wrong, and how to undo it — before the button, not after.

14Performance, reliability, privacy and responsiveness

Scanning

Brim's index is the app. Enumerate with getattrlistbulk, which returns metadata for many entries per call, rather than per-entry stat. Fetch size, dates, inode, link count and flags in the same pass so nothing needs a second visit. Persist the index in a local database, keep it current with filesystem events rather than rescanning, and bound concurrency to the number of performance cores so a background scan never makes the machine feel slow. Never read file contents except when hashing candidate duplicates, and then only after cheap filters have eliminated almost everything.

Recommendation Publish targets and hold to them: cold index under a minute on a typical machine, warm open under a second, no unbounded memory growth on a million-file volume, and no operation that blocks the interface.

Permissions

The permission ladder is a design problem, not a dialog. TCC blocks container access even from root;17 a privileged daemon established with SMAppService is needed for the system domain;16 and macOS 27 extends protection to some non-sandboxed apps' support folders.18

Recommendation Brim should run usefully with no elevated permissions at all, showing exactly what it cannot see and why, and ask for each capability at the moment it would change the result — never in an onboarding wall. A tool that demands Full Disk Access before showing anything is asking for maximum trust at minimum evidence, which is backwards for this product.

Privacy

No analytics, no accounts, no crash reporting that leaves the machine without an explicit per-incident decision, and no network traffic except user-initiated update checks (§6). This is not merely a value statement; it is the competitive position. It should be verifiable — the open engine, a documented list of every path Brim reads, and a build users can inspect.

Reliability

Recommendation The failure mode to engineer against is a partial operation: some items removed, some blocked by permissions, some failed. The ledger must record per-item outcomes, the interface must report the mixed result plainly, and the app bundle should be removed last so that a failed run can be retried after permissions are fixed rather than leaving an app that is gone but still registered.

What Brim should be

Brim is the record of what software has done to this Mac, and the safest way to undo it.

Not a cleaner. Cleaners compete on a number they cannot substantiate, against an incumbent with a $70M marketing budget, in a category where the free alternatives are good enough for the basics. That fight is unwinnable and not worth winning.

What is unsolved is adjudication. Existing tools find far more than users act on, because users cannot tell a real leftover from a shared dependency — and the documented cost of getting it wrong ranges from a corrupted creative suite21 to an unstable system with leftover daemons.2 Brim's product is the conversion from found to removed with confidence: every claim backed by a named kind of evidence, every consequential action reversible, every result verified against the filesystem afterwards.

Around that core sit three facts the OS makes visible only to people who use undocumented commands — what runs at login and who put it there, where storage actually went, and what software costs in battery over time. Brim's job is to make those legible and actionable, and to stop there.

Final V1 feature set

Scoped so a single maintainer can ship it and keep it alive — which, given what happened to both Pearcleaner and MacUpdater, is a product requirement rather than a constraint.

  • Application inventory with real footprint size, install date, signing and notarisation status, background registrations, and the privacy grants held.
  • Deep uninstall on the tiered evidence graph, with the shared-file veto, per-item capability checks, Trash-based removal, vendor-uninstaller routing, optional privacy-grant reset, and post-removal verification.
  • Leftovers, split into orphaned and unclaimed, with the ledger seeded on first run.
  • Reset an app to first-run state, and archive before removal.
  • Background and login items: what runs unasked, who registered it, whether its owner still exists, targeted removal where a backing file exists, and the guided reset with a captured restore list.
  • Storage: logical, reclaimable and snapshot-pinned shown separately; treemap; duplicates with clone-aware savings.
  • Energy: measured per-app energy, opt-in sampling, expressed as a share of a charge, with assertions and GPU context.
  • Developer cleanup on the three-class model.
  • Updates: detect via Sparkle feeds, Homebrew and the App Store; state the source per app; install by delegation only.
  • Migration hygiene view.
  • History: the removal ledger, browsable, undoable, exportable.
  • Explanations: deterministic, everywhere, by default.

V2 and later

  • Optional on-device model for summaries and natural-language search, with a bring-your-own-key path through the same provider protocol.15
  • Opt-in install observation, only if differential snapshots prove insufficient in practice — and only with its behaviour fully documented.
  • Standalone privacy-grant management across all apps.
  • Architecture stripping and language pruning, with signature re-verification.
  • Multi-user and multi-volume footprint consolidation.
  • Command-line interface over the same engine — cheap, and it reaches the audience that evangelises.
  • Adoption work on the uninstall-manifest specification: a validator, a build-phase helper, documentation.
  • Fleet reporting built on the exportable ledger, only if demand appears unprompted.

Rejected entirely

  • A curated per-app rules database. The cost structure that killed MacUpdater6 and it cannot explain itself.
  • Predicted battery life. Not defensible from available measurements; one wrong forecast costs more trust than the feature earns.
  • A cleanliness score or health percentage. Manufactured anxiety, and structurally incompatible with an evidence model.
  • Malware scanning. Permanent maintenance, duplicates XProtect, wrong product.
  • Memory purging and performance boosters. No mechanism.
  • System maintenance scripts and permission repair. Handled by macOS or folklore.2
  • Scheduled automatic deletion. Acting without adjudication is what Brim exists to replace.
  • An always-on install watcher in V1. Contradicts the trust position; readers say so directly.2
  • Browser and privacy cleaning as a headline feature. Commodity, breakage-prone.
  • An AI layer with authority over deletions.
  • Subscription pricing without a recurring cost to justify it.

Recommended UI/UX direction

A native inspector — sidebar, list, detail — with a ranked review queue as the landing surface and wizard flows for uninstall, reclaim and the background-items reset.

  • Evidence is never behind a click. Tier and reason are visible in the row.
  • Selection encodes confidence. Tier A and B pre-selected; C shown and selectable but never bulk-selected; shared items excluded with the other claimant named.
  • Every consequential screen states cost-of-error and undo path before the action.
  • Numbers are qualified or absent. Never a single figure that mixes reclaimable and pinned space.
  • Liquid Glass at the chrome, opacity and contrast at the data. Adopt the platform material where the platform provides it;29 keep dense tables legible.
  • Empty and blocked states give direction. "Full Disk Access would let me check 4 more locations" beats a silent gap — and macOS returns these failures silently.17
  • History is a permanent destination, not a dialog.

Technical and product principles

  1. Evidence before action. If Brim cannot name why a file belongs to an app, it does not select it.
  2. Prefer the owner's mechanism. Sparkle updates via Sparkle, Homebrew via brew, vendor uninstallers over guesses, tool caches via the tool's own command.
  3. Measure, do not estimate, where a measurement exists — and label an estimate as one where it does not.
  4. Verify after acting. Re-scan and measure the real free-space delta. Report zero honestly.
  5. Documented interfaces first, undocumented ones as enrichment only. Nothing consequential may depend on parsing output Apple does not specify.
  6. Degrade, never fail. Missing permissions, an unparseable format, an absent helper — reduce capability and say so.
  7. Determinism over models. Removing the model must cost polish only.
  8. No cost that scales with time. No database requiring daily human maintenance. This is why Brim can outlive MacUpdater.6
  9. Local by default. One network call class, user-initiated, disableable.
  10. Open what makes the safety claim. The rules are the argument; the signed build is the product.
  11. Scope for one maintainer. Every feature must be affordable in a week where nothing goes wrong.
